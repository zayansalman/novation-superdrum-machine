# Decoding 96 Drum Machines for Faithful Drum Racks

The 124 Splice folders resolve to **96 distinct machines**, and for 74 of them the research recovered the real panel layout, the sounds that shared a circuit and the per-voice controls at medium or high confidence, mostly from owner's manuals and period magazine reviews. That is enough to rebuild each pack as an Ableton Drum Rack whose pad order, choke groups, velocity behaviour and effects match the hardware. The rebuild rules turn out to be mechanical rather than a matter of taste: sounds that shared one voice channel (the Yamaha RX5's twelve key pairs, the Drumtraks' six channels, the Oberheim voice cards, the TR-505's groups) become choke groups; panel switches that swapped one sound for another (the TR-808's conga/tom switches, the KPR-77's cymbal/clap) become one pad with alternates; soft/medium/loud buttons (DMX, DX, LinnDrum) become velocity zones; and on most analog and early digital boxes accent was a single boost applied to every voice on a step. Only about 20 of the 96 had built-in effects, nearly all of them synths, workstations or 1990s units, so most racks should stay dry. The pattern side is much thinner. Named factory pattern lists exist for about 20 machines, but ready-to-type step grids were published for only three (Roland CR-8000, Boss DR-110, Korg DDM-110), and the CR-78 is the best route to famous grooves because Roland itself ties hits such as "Heart of Glass" and "In the Air Tonight" to named presets. No free, clearly licensed MIDI pattern files turned up for any machine; that is a weak negative, because the research ran out of web-search budget, but it means MIDI clips must for now be transcribed from manuals, built from preset lists, or taken by ear. The packs themselves need care: about a dozen folders are duplicates, mislabels, empty or unidentifiable, and none ships MIDI.

Machine-readable versions of everything below (voice lists, choke groups, note maps, preset names, sources) sit next to the research notes as JSON files in `~/research_notes/Vintage drum machine setups/`, one per maker family, which makes scripted rack building practical.

## Ninety-six machines hide behind 124 folders, and a dozen mislead

Most machines appear in two or three folders because the collection merges two sample sources. One source uses plain stereo names such as "Bassdrum-01", "Hat Closed" and "Tom H"; the other uses mono files prefixed with the machine name, such as "TR-808 Kick01" or "DMXSnare02". Where the two disagree, the prefixed set is usually the more complete one: the "TR-707" set has all 15 sounds while the plain set lacks the ride, and the "TR-727" set has all 15 while the plain one drops three ([local pack listing](file://~/Splice/sounds/packs)). Every folder holds 16-bit/44.1 kHz one-shots. **No folder contains MIDI**, and only three contain anything obviously rhythmic: twelve "Rhythm Ace LP" loops, one "512 loop.wav" in the MFB-512 folder, and a 3.4-second "Casio VL1 DaDaDa" phrase that may be the VL-1's Rock-1 preset heard on Trio's "Da Da Da".

Several folders are not what their names say. Cross-correlating the audio showed that "Akai Linndrum" is Linn LM-1/LinnDrum sample data replayed at 0.90–0.96 of the original speed, about 1.8 semitones low, which fits a 40 kHz-to-44.1 kHz transfer mismatch rather than separate recordings; "Alesis SR15" is a near-complete Alesis SR-16 ROM set (no SR-15 exists, every file is prefixed "SR16", and its category counts sit within one or two of the SR-16's published 49 kicks, 59 snares, 51 toms, 19 hats, 12 cymbals and 42 percussion) ([Music Technology, Feb 1991](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/alesis-sr16/776)); and the "Linn LM1/MT-32" subfolder is a 1998 Roland MT-32 sample set that should not be mapped as LM-1 sounds. Three folder pairs are exact copies: "Rhythm-Ace", "Sound Master, Memory Rhythm SR-88" and "MFB-512" are renamed, peak-normalised duplicates of "Ace Tone Rhythm Ace", "Sound Master" and "Fricke MFB512", with 10 ms of silence added at the end. Other packs contain sounds the hardware never had, including an open hat in the CR-78 set (the CR-78 has a single hi-hat voice) ([CR-78 manual](https://www.synthmanuals.com/manuals/roland/cr-78/owners_manual/roland_cr-78_owners_manual_rvgm.pdf)), a mid tom in the "CompuRhythm 8000" set (the CR-8000 has only low and high toms) ([CR-8000 manual](https://www.synthmanuals.com/manuals/roland/cr-8000/owners_manual/roland_cr-8000_owners_manual_rvgm.pdf)), and a "Hat_P" in the TR-606 set.

Some folders are broken or unidentifiable. All 32 "Kawai K3" files are 44-byte headers with no audio; the Korg T3 "Rim shot.wav" is empty, as are RM50 files FX_136–138. Four folders match no documented hardware: "Korg KRZ" (no such Korg product was found; its audio stops near 12.4 kHz, which hints at a mid-1980s digital machine sampling at about 25 kHz), "AJK Percussion Synth", "X Drum LM8953", and "Visco SpaceDrum", whose 40 files match a 40-sample entry in the KB6 archive, a source users describe as unreliably labelled ([KB6 downloads](https://samples.kb6.de/downloads.php); [GearSpace thread](https://gearspace.com/threads/visco-space-drum.390269/)). The "Korg Minipops" model is unknown, and the "Ace Tone Rhythm Ace" pack has hats, rim, bongos, conga and guiro that FR-1 sources never mention, so it probably comes from a later, larger Rhythm Ace. Six packs are numbered without names and will need an ear pass: "Yamaha CS6" (144 files against 145 drum and percussion waves), "Yamaha EX5" (126 non-empty files against exactly 126 drum waves), "Cheetah MD16" (48 files against 41 ROM sounds), "Quasimidi 309" (224 files not in manual order), and the two JD-800 folders, whose uploader admits having no name list. "Yamaha RM 50" is numbered by the RM50's preset voice banks, and its 65 cymbal files map one-for-one onto cymbal bank voices 1–65 with high confidence ([RM50 manual part 3](https://soundprogramming.net/manuals/drum/Yamaha_RM50_Manual3.pdf)).

## Shared circuits, not taste, decide chokes, velocity and swing

The most useful finding across all 96 machines is that the "feel" questions a rebuild raises (which sounds cut each other off, how accents behave, what the swing amounts were) were settled by how each machine allocated its hardware voices. Where several sounds share one playback channel, a new hit silences the old one, so a **choke group** is the faithful choice. The documented cases are explicit: the RX5's manual says that pressing both keys of a vertical pair "you will only hear one sound, because only one sound can be output from each of the RX5's 12 output channels" ([RX5 manual](https://soundprogramming.net/manuals/drum/Yamaha_RX5_Manual.pdf)); the Drumtraks manual says "only one instrument in each channel can be played simultaneously" across its six channels ([Drumtraks manual](https://vintagesynth.com/sites/default/files/2017-05/DrumtraksOperationsManual.pdf)); the Oberheim DX allows "only one voice variation" per voice ([DX manual](https://www.polynominal.com/site/studio/gear/drum/Oberheim_Dx/oberheim-dx-manual.pdf)); the Korg DDM-110 cannot play snare and rimshot, or open and closed hat, together, with snare and closed hat taking priority ([DDM-110 manual](https://www.korg.com/us/support/download/manual/1/371/3751/)); and on the TR-505, programming one sound in a group erases its partner ([Bonedo](https://www.bonedo.de/?p=530195)). Later machines made this a setting rather than a wiring fact: the R-8, SR-16, DM5, DR-550, DR-660, RM50, RY30, MPC60, JD-800, JD-990, D-70, CS6x and EX5 all have assignable exclusive, cut-off or mute groups, and their manuals or reviews mostly use closed/open hi-hat as the example. Equally telling are the machines with **no** choke mechanism: the Korg M1 and T-series kits and the Roland D-110 and MT-32 rhythm parts have no such parameter, so on those an open hat really did ring on through a closed one ([M1 manual](https://www.korg.com/us/support/download/manual/1/139/1685/); [D-110 manual](https://static.roland.com/assets/media/pdf/D-110_OM.pdf)).

Two other hardware habits translate directly. Panel switches that swapped one sound for another on the same circuit, such as the TR-808's three conga/tom switches and its claves/rimshot and maracas/clap selectors, the KPR-77's cymbal/clap switch and the KR-55's tom/conga pairs, are best built as one pad with switchable alternates (a Chain Selector) or two pads in a choke group, because the hardware could never play both. Fixed-level buttons, such as the DMX and DX's soft/medium/loud bass and snare or the LinnDrum's "Snare 1, 2 & 3", are the same sample at different levels and belong on velocity zones of one pad; Kenton's LinnDrum MIDI retrofit even documents the split points, 1–80/81–127 for two-level sounds and 1–30/31–80/81–127 for the three snare levels ([Kenton LinnDrum retrofit manual](https://kentonuk.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/LIN26000-20250619.pdf)).

Accent comes in three distinct forms, and getting this wrong is the commonest way a rebuilt groove sounds off. On the TR-808, TR-606, CR-78, CR-8000, DR-55, DR-110, DDM-110 and RX21, accent is a **separate step row that lifts every voice on that step**; the RX21 manual says pressing ACCENT on a beat "will affect ALL instruments sounding on that beat" ([RX21 manual](https://soundprogramming.net/manuals/drum/Yamaha_RX21_Manual.pdf)), and on the DR-110 the accent trigger drives a VCA on the whole mix ([Electric Druid](https://electricdruid.net/?p=71361)). In a MIDI clip, an accented step therefore raises the velocity of every note on that step. A second group stores accent **per voice**: the RX5 has two accent levels per key, the RX11 adds a per-instrument accent level to the base level, the Drumulator adds 0–15 to a 0–15 level without exceeding 15, the TR-626 allows ±3 per voice per step, and the MXR 185 applies a fixed internal accent per voice ([Drumulator manual](https://www.polynominal.com/emu-drumulator/emu-drumulator-manual.pdf); [Music Technology TR-626](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/roland-tr626/2125); [E&MM MXR 185](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/mxr-185-drum-computer/6203)). The Drumtraks quantifies its accent precisely: ACCENT "adds 80 final volume units" to a maximum unaccented 175, about 1.46 times ([Drumtraks manual](https://vintagesynth.com/sites/default/files/2017-05/DrumtraksOperationsManual.pdf)). A third group has **no accent**, only velocity or nothing: the Alesis HR-16, SR-16 and DM5, the Sequential TOM, the Ace Tone FR-1, the Sound Master SR-88 and the Yamaha MR-10.

Swing settings cluster on the same numbers across makers. The DMX runs "between 50% and 71% in six steps", the Drumulator offers 50, 54, 58, 63, 67 and 71%, the RX5 and RX11 54, 58, 63, 67 and 71%, the Drumtraks 50–70% in the same steps, and the Linn 9000 50–70% ([DMX manual](https://www.polynominal.com/oberheim-dmx/oberheim-dmx-manual.pdf); [Drumulator manual](https://www.polynominal.com/emu-drumulator/emu-drumulator-manual.pdf); [RX11 manual](https://soundprogramming.net/manuals/drum/Yamaha_RX11_Manual.pdf)). These figures are exactly 12 to 17 clock ticks out of the 24 in a pair of sixteenth notes at 48 pulses per quarter note, the clock rate the Drumulator manual says Linn products used; that arithmetic is this report's inference, but it means authentic swing amounts are whole 1/192-note nudges of the off-beat. One trap: the Drumtraks swings **eighth notes**, not sixteenths, and only with a quarter-note beat value. The MPC60 later widened the range to 50–75%, calling 66% "technically perfect" triplet swing ([MPC60 manual](https://www.polynominal.com/akai-mpc60/akai-mpc60-manual.pdf)).

Effects and note maps finish the picture. **Most racks should have no effects**: the analog boxes and nearly every 1980s drum machine had none, while the SR-16 and DM5 bake reverb and room ambience into many samples, which is why 91 of the "Alesis SR15" files are stereo ([Music Technology SR-16](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/alesis-sr16/776); [Sound On Sound DM5](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/alesis-dm5-0)). The machines that did have effects, such as the DR-660, DR-202, MC-303, D-110, MT-32, D-70, JD-800, JD-990, M1, T3, TG33, CS6x, EX5 and Quasimidi 309, mostly routed drums by per-pad send or per-key switch, which maps onto Drum Rack return chains. MIDI note maps converge on a General MIDI-like layout from the mid-1980s onward (the Oberheim DX manual already calls its map "an informal standard arrangement used by other drum machines"), with two important exceptions: the Yamaha RX5/RX11 family uses its own map with kicks on 44/45 and snares on 49/52, and the Akai XR10 interleaves its ten kicks across white keys. Note names are also unreliable across makers: Yamaha, Alesis and Ableton call MIDI note 36 "C1", while Roland calls it "C2" ([D-110 manual](https://static.roland.com/assets/media/pdf/D-110_OM.pdf)), so clips should be built from note numbers, never names.

## Machine by machine: what each real panel did

The tables below give, for every machine, the voices in front-panel (or pad, or key) order with the printed labels where a source shows them, the sounds that shared a voice or cut each other off, the per-voice controls with how accent and effects worked, and the MIDI note map plus what the pack folders hold. Abbreviations follow the hardware's own legends: BD bass drum, SD snare, RS rim shot, CH and OH closed and open hi-hat, CY cymbal, CB cowbell, CP or HCP hand clap. "Inferred" marks a conclusion the notes drew from indirect evidence rather than a source stating it; everything about pack contents comes from a read-only listing and audio analysis of the user's Splice folders.

### Roland TR rhythm composers: five switch pairs on the 808, near-GM note maps from the 909 on

Panel order is solid for the TR-808, TR-909 and TR-606, taken from Roland's own TR-08 and TR-09 recreation spec pages and a 1982 review; for the TR-707, 727, 505 and 626 the sound lists are certain but the printed abbreviations could not be checked because the original manual scans sit behind a bot wall. Two findings matter most for a build. On the 808, accent is effectively a silent "instrument" in the sequence that raises every other voice on the step ([iO-808 tutorial](https://io808.com/tutorial)), and its tone, decay, snappy and tuning knobs were never programmable, so a faithful rack treats them as fixed per kit. And the four MIDI-era TR boxes share one note layout close to the later General MIDI drum map (kit on 35–51, tambourine and cowbell on 54/56, Latin on 60–75), so a single Drum Rack layout covers the 707, 727, 505 and 626 with almost no collisions ([Zoë Blade, citing Roland manuals and service notes](https://notebook.zoeblade.com/Roland_TR_MIDI_map.html)). None of the nine had built-in effects.

### Roland CompuRhythm, R-series and MC boxes: the CR-8000 manual charts every preset

Two scanned Roland owner's manuals, for the CR-78 (1980 print) and CR-8000 (1983 print), were read page by page and anchor this family. The CR-78 has 11 programmable voices plus three "add voices" that play only from sliders, a global accent and a BALANCE slider that tilts the mix between high sounds (cymbal, hat, maracas) and the bass drum; it documents no chokes at all, and Attack Magazine's CR-78-sample beat deliberately uses "no chokes or mute groups" ([Attack Magazine](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/cr-78-redux/)). The CR-8000 groups its sounds onto six channels with shared level knobs and prints an official step chart for every preset, which makes it the single most rebuildable pattern machine in the collection. The R-8 and R-5 move to assignable exclusive groups and per-instrument pitch, decay and "nuance", while the MC-303 and MC-09 are the only members with effects.

### Boss Dr. Rhythm: from a four-voice analog box to GM-style 55-note kits

### Linn, Oberheim and E-mu: voice cards and level buttons define the early digital sound

The Oberheim DMX, Oberheim DX and E-mu Drumulator owner's manuals fully document their panels; the LM-1 and LinnDrum layouts come mostly from Kenton's MIDI-retrofit manuals because Linn's own manuals were unreadable online. These machines encode dynamics as separate fixed-level buttons and share hardware aggressively: the DMX has eight voice cards of three sounds each, the DX six voices of which "only one voice variation can be played at a time", and the Linn 9000's four toms are two samples, so only two can sound at once ([DMX manual](https://www.polynominal.com/oberheim-dmx/oberheim-dmx-manual.pdf); [DX manual](https://www.polynominal.com/site/studio/gear/drum/Oberheim_Dx/oberheim-dx-manual.pdf); [ES&CM 1985](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/the-beating-heart/3428)). None had user effects, but two had fixed filtering that colours the sound: the SP-12's outputs 1–2 pass through dynamic 4-pole low-pass filters that close as the sound decays, and later LM-1s run the bass, toms and congas through a VCF ([CCRMA](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~nolting/220c); [vLinn manual](https://www.alyjameslab.com/exclusive/VLINN_Linn_LM-1Drums_USER_MANUAL_V2.pdf)).

### Korg: priority rules on the DDM-110, one switched voice on the KPR-77, no chokes on the workstations

Korg's DDM-110 manual is unusually explicit about voice sharing, stating that snare beats rimshot and closed hat beats open hat whenever both land together, and that plugging anything into the TRIG OUT jack silences the claps ([DDM-110 manual](https://www.korg.com/us/support/download/manual/1/371/3751/)). On the KR-55, circuit analysis shows the closed-hat trigger resetting the open hat and each conga sharing its circuit with a tom ([Electric Druid](https://electricdruid.net/?p=72032)). The M1 and T-series workstation kits have official numbered sound lists but no published factory key maps and no exclusive groups, and their drums reach the effects only when panned to outputs A or B.

### Yamaha RX family: twelve choke pairs on the RX5, its own non-GM note map

Yamaha's RX manuals, image-only scans read by OCR, give complete charts, and they make the RX5 the clearest example of hardware-defined chokes in the collection: each vertical key pair is one of twelve output channels, so each pair is a choke group, open and closed hat included ([RX5 manual](https://soundprogramming.net/manuals/drum/Yamaha_RX5_Manual.pdf)). The RX5 and RX11 default notes are their own scheme rather than General MIDI, with kicks on 44/45, snares on 49/52 and the closed hat on 57, so GM-mapped MIDI files need re-mapping; the TG33 drum kit sits in the middle of that same scheme ([TG33 manual part 1](https://soundprogramming.net/manuals/Yamaha_TG33_Manual1.pdf)). None of the RX units or the MR-10 had effects.

### Early rhythm boxes and synths: presets without programming, drums without drum voices

The four drum boxes here have almost no per-voice controls, and the four synths have no drum voices at all, so their packs are patches rather than kits. The documented hardware chokes are the MFB-512's toms, which are one sample so only one can play at a time, and its hats, which cut the cymbal's decay ([E&MM 1984](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/mfb-digital-drum-machine/7888)). On the Sound Master SR-88, the pack's "long" snare and hat are the same circuits held on by keeping a step button pressed, so each long sound should choke with its short partner ([Rhythmic Robot](https://www.rhythmicrobot.com/?p=811)). None of the eight had reverb, delay or compression built in.

### Simmons, MXR and Sakata: one analog voice per module, a 4×3 grid, and accent as a separate sound

The Simmons SDS-V (sold as SDS 5, the "V" being a Roman numeral) is a set of pad-played analog modules, one voice each, with four memories per module, one of them a fixed factory sound; every sample from a module is therefore a different setting of one channel and belongs on one pad with switchable alternates ([Music UK 1983](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/simmons-sds-5-electronic-drums-kit-and-sds-6-sequencer/12013)). ## Grooves: three manuals hand over step grids, and the CR-78 hands over the hits

Factory patterns are where the documentation thins out. Only three sources print patterns as ready-to-type step grids: the CR-8000 manual charts every preset, arranger pattern and fill with "● = ♪, No Mark = rest" ([CR-8000 manual](https://www.synthmanuals.com/manuals/roland/cr-8000/owners_manual/roland_cr-8000_owners_manual_rvgm.pdf)); the DR-110 manual's "Sample Rhythm" pages give example grids to type in, which are not its ROM presets but are the closest published DR-110 grooves ([DR-110 manual](https://www.theninhotline.com/dr110/Boss-dr110-manual.pdf)); and the DDM-110 manual prints one two-bar example pattern ([DDM-110 manual](https://www.korg.com/us/support/download/manual/1/371/3751/)). The research transcribed eight CR-8000 charts (each checked against the staff notation printed above it), eight DR-110 grids and the DDM-110 example, which together are enough for a first set of MIDI clips. The DR-110 grids leave out the manual's optional marks: an open hat on steps 4 and 10 in ROCK II, an optional cymbal in ROCK III, hand claps on steps 5 and 13 in ELECTRONIC POP I and DISCO, a cymbal on step 1 to start a song, and a snare-plus-accent fill on steps 15 and 16 in several patterns. ELECTRONIC POP II also marks an accent on the otherwise empty step 9.

Several more sources hold exact patterns in a form that needs careful transcription rather than copying. The CR-78 manual prints all 34 presets and seven fills in drum-staff notation, too small in the available scan to transcribe reliably ([CR-78 manual](https://www.synthmanuals.com/manuals/roland/cr-78/owners_manual/roland_cr-78_owners_manual_rvgm.pdf)); Korg's own KPR-77 rhythm charts print its 48 preprogrammed pattern slots (A-11 is blank) as staff notation, recommending a tempo of "6-8 on the dial" ([Korg KPR-77 charts](https://www.korg.com/us/support/download/manual/1/129/2119/)); the DR-110 manual notates its 16 presets C-1 to D-8; and the TR-808 and TR-606 manuals contain "Sample Rhythm" sections that sat behind bot-check walls ([ManualsLib TR-808](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/695754/Roland-Tr-808.html); [TR-606 manual contents](https://all-guidesbox.com/manual/1001808/roland-tr-606-owner-s-manual-48.html)). Two pattern booklets known to exist were not found anywhere: the Boss DR-550 "Preset Pattern Scores" and the Yamaha "RX21 Pattern Book". The famous-recording evidence follows the same pattern: rich for a few machines, thin elsewhere, and **no published step chart of any famous groove was found** for any of the 96. The single most valuable link is the CR-78, because Roland's own history ties specific hits to specific presets: Blondie's "Heart of Glass" combines the Mambo and Beguine presets, Phil Collins's "In the Air Tonight" uses Disco 2 in its A variation, Hall & Oates's "I Can't Go for That" uses Rock 1, Ultravox's "Vienna" uses Rock 4, and Chris de Burgh's "The Lady in Red" uses Enka ([Roland, "CR-78: The Whole Story"](https://articles.roland.com/?p=75463)). Those grooves are therefore already inside the CR-78 manual's notation. The Mini Pops 7 and VL-1 offer the same shortcut: combining the Mini Pops BEGUINE and SLOW ROCK presets "gets the classic Oxygene IV rhythm" ([KVR POPS-7](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/pops-7-by-forgotten-keys)), and Trio's "Da Da Da" reportedly uses the VL-1's stock Rock-1 rhythm ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casio_VL-1)). Attack Magazine's "Beat Dissected" series is the nearest thing to published grids, but it shows steps only as images and never pins a groove to a specific machine; its Cybotron "Clear" rebuild uses an 808 kit at 125 BPM with 50% swing, and its "CR-78 Redux" beat runs at 120–125 BPM with 53–55% swing ([Attack, Cybotron "Clear"](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/how-to-make-an-electro-beat-inspired-by-cybotrons-clear/); [Attack, CR-78 Redux](https://www.attackmagazine.com/technique/beat-dissected/cr-78-redux/)).

No free, clearly licensed MIDI pattern files were found for any machine, and every research team flagged this as an unfinished search rather than a proven absence. What does exist is commercial or indirect: Samples From Mars' "MR10 From Mars" includes four MIDI grooves based on the MR-10 patterns ([Samples From Mars](https://samplesfrommars.com/products/mr10-from-mars)); the Forgotten Keys POPS-7 plug-in recreates the Mini Pops rhythms and records them to MIDI ([KVR](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/pops-7-by-forgotten-keys)); AudioThing's SR-88 lets its patterns be dragged into a DAW as MIDI ([AudioThing](https://www.audiothing.net/instruments/sr-88/)); and the Drumtraks manual fully documents its SysEx pattern format, so a factory-memory dump could in principle be decoded into MIDI ([Drumtraks manual](https://vintagesynth.com/sites/default/files/2017-05/DrumtraksOperationsManual.pdf)). Licensing deserves one note: Quasimidi's manual explicitly allows its preset patterns in your own productions while forbidding redistribution ([309 manual](https://www.vintagesynth.com/sites/default/files/2017-05/309Man.pdf)), and the only sample pack with stated terms is the "SH09 Drum sounds" folder, whose 1997 readme declares the samples free of copyright.

## Conclusion

The rebuild is mostly a data problem, not a sound-design problem. About a dozen hardware behaviours (shared channels, switched alternates, fixed-level buttons, global versus per-voice accent, assignable exclusive groups, 48-ppqn swing steps, per-pad effect sends) explain how all 96 machines behaved, and the structured JSON beside the notes already records them per machine, so racks can be generated by script rather than hand-built 124 times. The biggest threats to fidelity are not sample quality but the packs' own labelling, meaning extras the hardware never had, renamed duplicates, slowed transfers and unnamed numbered sets, and the easy mistake of giving each voice its own accent on machines where accent lifted the whole step. Five gaps remain where the manuals were unreadable online: the printed labels of the TR-707, 727, 505 and 626, the LM-1 and LinnDrum button legends, the Linn 9000 and SP-12 default pad layouts, the RZ-1 button order, and the factory key maps of the M1, T3, D-70 and JD-800/JD-990 kits.

For the MIDI clips, the order of work follows the evidence. The CR-8000, DR-110 and DDM-110 grids can be typed in today. The CR-78 and Mini Pops are next, because their famous grooves are documented combinations of named presets, which turns "recreate a classic record" into "transcribe one manual page". The KPR-77 charts and the DR-110 presets follow, as staff notation from the manufacturers. Everything else needs either a new search for licensed MIDI, the cheapest open question since every team ran out of search budget before reaching it, or transcription by ear, and any clip made that way should be labelled as a reconstruction rather than a factory pattern.
